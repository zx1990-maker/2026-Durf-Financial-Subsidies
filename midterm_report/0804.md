# Part I. Full English Translation

## Executive Summary

This study uses all privately owned A-share listed companies as the training sample to generate counterfactual forecasts of EBI/A, an asset-return measure, for state-owned enterprises (SOEs) included in the CSI 500 and CSI 1000 indices. The purpose is to test whether SOEs receive systematic government financial support. After four rigorous diagnostic tests—systematic-bias testing, common-support testing, placebo testing, and bootstrap confidence intervals—the main result is that, after imposing the common-support restriction, the subsidy gap for the combined CSI 500 and CSI 1000 sample is +2.09 percentage points, with a 95% confidence interval of \[+1.28, +3.02\]. The estimate is statistically positive, but its economic magnitude is much smaller than commonly assumed. The gap is highly concentrated among loss-making centrally controlled IT-service SOEs and in capital-intensive industries. In addition, only 9.6% of CSI 500 SOEs have adequate common support.

# 1\. Research Background and Project Evolution

## 1.1 Research Question

Core question: If a Chinese SOE were instead a privately owned company, while keeping the same financial characteristics and industry conditions, what would its EBI/A be? Can the difference between the actual value and the counterfactual forecast be interpreted as government financial support?

## 1.2 Project Evolution: Why Earlier Approaches Did Not Produce a Satisfactory Answer

The project went through three stages of model development.

Ten experiments were conducted, including multi-metric robustness checks, cross-market transportability tests, placebo tests, five matching or weighting methods, rolling-window out-of-time validation, an asset-beta experiment, and a benchmark based on Chinese privately owned companies listed in Hong Kong.

*   Initial pooled Ridge: +2.33 pp
*   After removing CSI non-SOEs: +0.75 pp
*   After switching to Random Forest: +0.19 pp

*   After removing leverage values above 2: -0.59 pp
*   After correcting the asset-turnover formula: -0.60 pp

1.  Benchmark sensitivity:

*   Private H-share benchmark: +0.75 pp
*   Private CSI 300 benchmark: -0.41 pp
*   Chinese private firms listed in Hong Kong, 595 firms: -6.06 pp, with the opposite sign
*   Within the information-technology industry: Chinese private-company gap = -9.03 pp, versus -0.07 pp using seven years of H-share data
*   The estimated gap ranged from -6 pp to +0.75 pp depending on the benchmark
*   The project’s own conclusion was that no benchmark was methodologically superior to the others

1.  SOEs and private firms had almost no overlap in observable characteristics:

*   Firm size differed by 2.6 standard deviations.
*   Entropy balancing did not converge because no positive set of weights could simultaneously match the SOE and private-firm feature means.
*   The effective sample size under overlap weighting was only 36, or 2%.
*   After propensity-score matching, the standardized mean difference remained 0.56, far above the target of 0.1.
*   After coarsened exact matching by year and industry, the standardized mean difference was still 1.56.
*   Only 3%, or 4 of 154 SOEs, were inside the propensity-score common-support region.
*   Therefore, 97% of the estimated gap came from extrapolation rather than interpolation.

1.  The training sample was too small:

*   The CSI 300 private-firm sample contained only about 98 firms per year over seven years, or roughly 685 observations.
*   That was insufficient for reliable same-market prediction with nine features plus industry fixed effects.

1.  The H-share benchmark itself was deteriorating:

*   EBI/A for non-SOE H-share firms fell from +5.0% in 2010 to +0.6% in 2024, a decline of 4.4 pp.
*   The benchmark was therefore a moving target.

The project’s final conclusion, quoted from 项目进展总结.md, was: “The gap lies between -6 pp and +0.75 pp depending on the chosen benchmark. No benchmark is clearly superior on methodological grounds. SOEs and private firms belong to two almost non-overlapping populations in observable characteristics.”

The final report explicitly recommended that, under the existing framework, the SOE gap should not be interpreted as evidence of government financial support.

### Stage 2: K-Nearest-Neighbors Matching (Abandoned)

Path: /模型/KNN\_HK/

Method: Privately owned H-share firms were used as the donor pool. K-nearest-neighbors matching, based on Mahalanobis distance or a similar metric, directly matched CSI 500 A-share SOEs to comparable firms. The mean EBI/A of the nearest neighbors was used as the counterfactual forecast. A caliper of approximately 1.5 to 1.7 and a minimum of 10 donors were required.

Reasons for failure: extremely low match rates.

*   Only 50 of 179 CSI 500 SOEs, or 28%, were successfully matched.
*   For most of the 129 unmatched firms, fewer than 10 comparable H-share private firms were available.
*   For other firms, the nearest-neighbor distance exceeded the caliper. Some distances were extreme—for example, 105,555 for 300140.SZ and 50,000 for 600292.SH—showing that no comparable firms existed in the H-share market.
*   The method had no model-training capability and could not use the full information in the A-share market.
*   Cross-market matching remained inherently limited because H-share and A-share financial characteristics were not comparable.
*   Only result CSV files remained and no code was retained, indicating that the approach was abandoned early.

### Stage 3: Linear-A, the Current Model — Why It Works

Path: /模型/Linear-A/

Key breakthroughs:

1.  A training sample covering all private A-share firms: 27,722 valid observations, 3,471 private companies, and an eleven-year panel from 2014 to 2024.

*   The training sample is forty times larger than the private-firm sample from the CSI 300.
*   Prediction is conducted within the same market, A-share to A-share, eliminating the earlier cross-market transportability problem.

1.  Expansion to the CSI 500 and CSI 1000, rather than limiting the analysis to approximately 118 CSI 300 SOEs.

*   The target sample includes 178 CSI 500 SOEs and 331 CSI 1000 SOEs.
*   This addresses the limitation that the CSI 300 contains mainly large-cap companies.

1.  A rigorous four-part diagnostic framework: systematic bias, common support, placebo testing, and bootstrap inference.

*   The first two project stages did not apply this diagnostic framework systematically.

## 7.2 Core Assessment

1.  The evidence supports a subsidy gap that is significantly greater than zero.

*   The bootstrap 95% confidence intervals do not include zero.
*   The placebo-test p-value is 0.005.
*   The OOF bias is close to zero.

1.  However, the economic magnitude is much smaller than traditionally believed.

*   The bias-corrected gap for index constituents is approximately +2.2 to +2.5 pp.
*   The gap for all A-share SOEs is approximately +1.5 pp.
*   The reliable estimates are far below interpretations based on the earlier pooled Ridge result, whose +2.33 pp estimate was shown to be a market\_A artifact.

1.  Insufficient common support is the main caveat.

*   Only 26.5% of SOEs have common support, and only 9.6% of CSI 500 SOEs do.
*   After restricting the analysis to the common-support sample, the gap remains positive at +2.09 pp.
*   Fourteen industries are completely excluded. Estimates for industries such as steel and oil and petrochemicals are therefore unreliable.

1.  The model has no systematic bias.

*   The OOF mean error is +0.0001, and the calibration results are consistent with perfect calibration.

1.  The gap is highly concentrated among loss-making centrally controlled IT-service companies and capital-intensive industries.

*   Examples include software services, with a common-support gap of +7.11 pp; steel, with a full-sample gap of +6.67 pp; and oil and petrochemicals, with +5.53 pp.
*   These gaps may reflect policy burdens—such as maintaining production and supply or supporting national digitalization—rather than direct financial support.

1.  Index selection materially affects the estimated gap.

*   CSI 300, large cap: -0.68 pp.
*   CSI 500 and CSI 1000, mid and small cap: approximately +2.6 to +2.8 pp.
*   All A-share SOEs: +1.53 pp.
*   Selection based on capitalization and liquidity systematically amplifies the gap among index constituents.

## 7.3 Methodological Contributions

1.  The study replaces approximately 98 private CSI 300 firms with 3,471 private A-share firms, increasing the training sample by a factor of forty and solving the same-market sample-size problem.
2.  It uses a strict OOF framework with GroupKFold by firm\_id, and all model selection is completed within the private-firm sample.
3.  It applies four diagnostics systematically: bias, common support, placebo testing, and bootstrap inference.
4.  It treats common support as a core screening tool, identifies fourteen industries with no valid benchmark, and clearly defines the boundaries within which the gap can be interpreted.

## 7.4 Limitations and Next Steps

1.  Only 17 CSI 500 SOEs remain in the common-support sample. The CSI 500 result therefore depends heavily on extrapolation.
2.  The placebo and bootstrap repetitions, currently 200 to 500, should be increased to 1,000 to 2,000 for more stable confidence intervals.
3.  The current analysis is a one-period forecast from 2024 to 2025. It should be extended to a multi-year panel forecast to use the time dimension.
4.  Ridge imposes linearity, while GBM has a higher cross-validated R-squared, +0.313 versus +0.206. Nonlinear gap estimates deserve further investigation.
5.  The gaps in excluded industries, including steel and oil and petrochemicals, cannot be validated within the common-support framework. A new methodology is required for those sectors.

# Part II. English Presentation Script

The script below is written for a clear, direct presentation. It follows the report section by section and keeps the most important numbers.

## Opening

Good morning, everyone. Today I will present our study on the counterfactual EBI/A of Chinese state-owned enterprises, or SOEs. Our main question is simple: if an SOE had the same financial and industry characteristics but operated as a private company, what return on assets should we expect? We then compare that counterfactual value with the company’s actual EBI/A. We call the difference the subsidy gap.

The main result is that the gap is statistically positive, but smaller than many people may expect. For the CSI 500 and CSI 1000 firms that have common support, the estimated gap is 2.09 percentage points. The 95% confidence interval is from 1.28 to 3.02 percentage points. However, the result is concentrated in a small number of loss-making central SOEs and capital-intensive industries. Common support is also weak, especially in the CSI 500.

## Section 1: Research Background and Project Evolution

Let me first explain why this project went through several model versions. The early question was the same, but the benchmark was not reliable.

In the first stage, we used three benchmarks: private H-share firms, private CSI 300 firms, and a pooled sample. The estimated gaps ranged from negative 0.41 percentage points to positive 2.33 percentage points. This large range showed that the answer depended too much on the benchmark.

The main problem was cross-market transportability. H-share firms and A-share firms were too different. The H-share model could not even predict private A-share firms accurately. Once we separated the two markets and corrected the data and model issues, the original pooled gap fell from 2.33 percentage points to negative 0.60 percentage points, with a confidence interval that included zero.

The second stage used K-nearest-neighbor matching with H-share private firms. This also failed because only 28% of CSI 500 SOEs could be matched. For many SOEs, there were not enough comparable private firms, or the nearest firms were still extremely far away.

The third stage, called Linear-A, solves the main problems. We use all private A-share companies as the training sample. This gives us 27,722 observations from 3,471 firms between 2014 and 2024. It is forty times larger than the earlier CSI 300 private-firm sample, and it keeps the prediction within the same market.

## Section 2: Model Setup

The model uses 2024 financial characteristics to predict 2025 EBI/A. EBI/A is calculated as net profit attributable to the parent plus interest expense, divided by total assets.

We use nine financial and industry variables: firm size, asset turnover, financial leverage, fixed-assets ratio, current ratio, capital-expenditure ratio, working-capital ratio, market share, and industry concentration. We also include detailed industry fixed effects.

Our main model is Ridge regression with alpha equal to 10. We use median imputation, standardization, and winsorization at the 5th and 95th percentiles. Cross-validation is grouped by firm, so the same company cannot appear in both the training and validation parts of a fold.

Most importantly, all model choices are made only with private-firm data. We do not use actual SOE performance to tune the model. This prevents data leakage.

We also tested Random Forest and Gradient Boosting. They had higher predictive R-squared values, especially Gradient Boosting, but Ridge remained the main model because it is easier to interpret and is consistent with the earlier research design.

## Section 3: Main Results

The results differ strongly across indices. The CSI 300 gap is negative 0.68 percentage points. This means large-cap SOEs perform below the private-firm counterfactual benchmark.

For the CSI 500, the mean gap is positive 2.82 percentage points. For the CSI 1000, it is positive 2.59 percentage points. For the combined CSI 500 and CSI 1000 sample, it is positive 2.67 percentage points.

When we expand the analysis to all 1,394 A-share SOEs, the gap falls to 1.53 percentage points. This tells us that index selection matters. The CSI 500 and CSI 1000 constituents show a larger gap than the full SOE population.

## Section 4: Four Diagnostic Tests

### Diagnostic 1: Systematic Bias

First, we test whether the model has a systematic prediction bias. Using strict out-of-fold predictions for private firms, the mean error is almost exactly zero: 0.0001. The p-value is 0.923, and the calibration intercept and slope are not statistically different from zero and one.

This means the model is well calibrated in the private-firm sample. For private firms that look most like SOEs, the model actually predicts slightly too low. Therefore, a positive SOE gap cannot be explained by the model simply overpredicting SOE returns.

### Diagnostic 2: Common Support

Second, we test whether SOEs and private firms are truly comparable. This is the biggest limitation of the study.

SOEs are much larger, have higher market shares, operate in more concentrated industries, and hold more fixed assets. A propensity-score model can distinguish SOEs from private firms with an AUC of 0.925. This means the two groups are very different.

Only 26.5% of all SOEs are in the good-support region. For the CSI 500, the figure is only 9.6%. In other words, most CSI 500 estimates rely on extrapolation.

However, after restricting the sample to firms with common support, the combined gap is still positive at 2.09 percentage points. Under several support thresholds, the result stays between about 1.9 and 3.1 percentage points. So the sign is stable, even though the coverage is limited.

### Diagnostic 3: Placebo Test

Third, we run placebo tests. We take private firms with the same industry distribution as the SOEs, remove them from the training sample, retrain the model, and predict them. We repeat this 200 times.

The average placebo gap is only 0.28 percentage points for the CSI 500 design and 0.38 percentage points for the CSI 1000 design. The real gaps are more than seven standard deviations above the placebo means. The empirical p-value is 0.005.

After correcting for the small placebo bias, the gap remains 2.54 percentage points for the CSI 500 and 2.21 percentage points for the CSI 1000.

### Diagnostic 4: Bootstrap Confidence Intervals

Fourth, we use a firm-level cluster bootstrap with 500 full model refits. The 95% confidence intervals are positive in all main samples.

For the CSI 500, the interval is 2.06 to 3.37 percentage points. For the CSI 1000, it is 1.85 to 3.01 percentage points. For the common-support combined sample, it is 1.28 to 3.02 percentage points. None of these intervals includes zero.

## Section 5: Deeper Common-Support Results

Common-support screening improves the alignment of the samples. For example, leverage and industry concentration become almost identical between private firms and common-support SOEs. Fixed-asset ratios, liquidity, and working capital also move much closer.

But fourteen industries have no common-support SOEs at all. These include steel, oil and petrochemicals, real estate, and coal. For these industries, we cannot find valid private-firm comparisons, so their gap estimates are not reliable.

These excluded industries often have large positive gaps. Steel has a gap of 6.67 percentage points, and oil and petrochemicals has 5.53 percentage points. Because these firms are outside common support, they increase the full-sample gap.

At the company level, the largest positive gaps are concentrated among firms with severe actual losses. Examples include Guoxin Health, SDIC Intelligence, and Efort. By contrast, firms with large negative gaps are highly profitable companies such as Zhaojin Gold, Dahao Technology, and New Media.

This pattern is important. A large positive gap may reflect policy burdens, weak operating performance, or strategic tasks, not only financial subsidies.

## Section 6: All A-Share SOEs

When we extend the target sample to all A-share SOEs, the average gap is 1.53 percentage points. After applying the common-support restriction, it is almost unchanged at 1.52 percentage points.

This is a very stable result, and it is much smaller than the gap among CSI 500 and CSI 1000 constituents. It suggests that index rules based on market value and liquidity amplify the observed gap.

We also find only a small difference between central and local SOEs. The common-support gap is 1.23 percentage points for central SOEs and 1.63 percentage points for local SOEs.

## Section 7: Final Interpretation

Our final interpretation has two parts.

First, the statistical evidence supports a positive gap. The model has almost no overall bias, the placebo gaps are near zero, and the bootstrap confidence intervals do not include zero.

Second, we should be careful about the economic meaning. The gap is around 2.2 to 2.5 percentage points for the index samples after bias correction, and around 1.5 percentage points for all A-share SOEs. This is meaningful, but it is not extremely large.

The most important caveat is common support. Only about one quarter of SOEs have good support, and the CSI 500 result is based mostly on extrapolation. Fourteen industries cannot be validated under the current framework.

The gap is also highly concentrated in loss-making central IT-service companies and heavy industries. These companies may carry policy responsibilities, such as maintaining production, ensuring supply, or supporting national information systems. Therefore, the gap should not automatically be treated as a pure measure of government financial support.

The most defensible conclusion is this: Chinese SOEs show a statistically positive counterfactual EBI/A gap, but the average economic magnitude is modest, the result is sensitive to index composition, and interpretation is limited by weak common support and industry-specific policy burdens.

## Methodological Contributions

This project makes four main methodological contributions. First, it uses 3,471 private A-share companies instead of about 98 private CSI 300 firms. Second, it uses strict firm-level out-of-fold validation. Third, it combines four diagnostic tests in one framework. Fourth, it uses common support to identify where the estimates are credible and where they are not.

## Limitations and Next Steps

There are five clear next steps. We should increase the placebo and bootstrap repetitions to at least 1,000. We should extend the analysis from one year to a multi-year panel. We should study nonlinear models because Gradient Boosting predicts better than Ridge. We need a new method for industries with no common support. Finally, we should treat the CSI 500 estimate carefully because only 17 firms remain in its common-support sample.

## Closing

To conclude, our study finds a positive but moderate SOE counterfactual gap. The result survives several diagnostic tests, but it should not be interpreted as a simple or universal measure of government subsidy. The evidence is strongest for the common-support sample and weakest for industries where no comparable private firms exist. Thank you.
